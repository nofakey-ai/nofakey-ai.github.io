How to Check If a Dating Profile Is Fake

6 min read Relationship safety

The conversation feels easy in a way that surprises you. They're attentive, they remember details, they seem genuinely interested — more than you expected from someone you matched with a few days ago. It's a nice feeling. It's also, occasionally, exactly the feeling a fake profile is built to create.

Most people on dating and matrimonial platforms are exactly who they say they are. But knowing what a fake profile tends to look like is worth ten minutes, because the cost of missing one isn't just a bad match — it can be a long, expensive con.

1. Look closely at their photos

Profiles using only a small number of photos, all clearly professional or model-quality, are worth a second look. Try a reverse image search on one or two of their pictures — if the same photos show up under a different name elsewhere online, you have your answer.

2. Notice how fast the relationship is moving

Declarations of strong feelings within days, talk of a shared future very early, or language that feels intense before you've even had a real conversation are a pattern often called love bombing. It feels flattering. It's also a well-documented way to short-circuit the normal pace of getting to know someone.

3. Ask for a video call, and notice how they respond

A real person will generally be willing to video call eventually, even if a little shy about it at first. Repeated excuses — a broken camera, bad internet, being "too busy," always tomorrow — especially over an extended period, is one of the clearest signs the person isn't who their photos suggest.

4. Watch for a story that doesn't quite hold together

Common narratives include working abroad, being in the military, being an engineer on an oil rig, or being suddenly widowed. None of these are impossible on their own, but they're extremely common in fake profiles because they conveniently explain away video calls, in-person meetings, and slow responses.

5. Be alert the moment money enters the conversation

A medical emergency, a stuck shipment, travel costs to finally meet you, a "temporary" cash flow problem — once any version of a money request appears, even a small or sympathetic one, treat it as a serious signal, regardless of how genuine the relationship has felt up to that point.

Quick rule of thumb: a real relationship can survive you taking time to verify someone's identity. If asking for a video call or a bit of patience causes anger, guilt-tripping, or a sudden crisis, that reaction is itself a red flag.

6. Trust a slow, cautious pace

There's no rush in getting to know someone properly, whether the platform is a dating app or a matrimonial site. Meeting in a public place once video-verified, involving family or friends in the process where appropriate, and taking your time are all reasonable, unremarkable choices — and a genuine match will be comfortable with all of them.

A quick checklist before you trust a profile

  • Do their photos check out under a reverse image search?
  • Is the relationship moving unusually fast, emotionally?
  • Have they consistently avoided or delayed a video call?
  • Does their story include convenient reasons why meeting or verifying is difficult?
  • Has money, a gift, or financial help come up in any form?

More than one "yes" here is worth slowing down for, however genuine the connection feels.

Conclusion

What's described above is widely documented as catfishing — creating a false online identity to build trust, usually for emotional or financial gain. Recognizing the pattern doesn't mean assuming the worst about everyone you meet online; it just means giving yourself the same thoughtful pace you'd want in any real relationship.

Frequently asked questions

Is it catfishing if someone just uses an old photo of themselves?

Not necessarily. Catfishing specifically means using someone else's identity or a stolen photo to deceive you, not simply using an outdated picture of the same real person.

Why won't a fake profile do a video call?

Because a video call would immediately reveal that the person doesn't match their photos. A consistent refusal to video chat, especially with excuses that keep changing, is one of the most reliable signs of a fake profile.

Is it a scam if they haven't asked for money yet?

It can still be heading that way. Trust-building often happens for weeks before any financial request appears, specifically so the eventual ask doesn't feel sudden or suspicious.

What should I do if I think I'm talking to a fake profile?

Stop sharing personal or financial information, avoid sending money or gifts, and consider doing a reverse image search on their photos to check where else those images appear online.
